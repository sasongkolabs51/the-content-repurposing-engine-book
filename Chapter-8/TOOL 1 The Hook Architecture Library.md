## TOOL 1: The Hook Architecture Library

**Purpose:** A catalog of specific, AI-ready hook patterns for each platform. Each pattern includes the structural template, a "Do" example, a "Don't" example, and the prompt instruction you paste into your template. This replaces vague "write a strong hook" instructions with precision engineering.

**Time needed:** 30 minutes to customize for your niche. Then it's a permanent reference.

---

### TWITTER / X HOOK ARCHITECTURES

---

#### Pattern 1: The Bold Reframe

**The mechanism:** State a widely accepted belief, then invert it in the same sentence or the next. The reader's brain goes "wait, what?" and needs to resolve the contradiction.

**Structure:**
`[Common belief]. [Inversion that implies a larger truth the reader must click to access.]`

**Do example:**
"Most productivity advice assumes you want to be productive. That's the mistake."

**Don't example:**
"Have you ever thought about productivity differently?" (Too vague. No inversion. No tension.)

**Prompt instruction (paste into your Twitter prompt):**

```
HOOK PATTERN — BOLD REFRAME:
Open with a single sentence (under 140 characters) that states a
widely accepted belief in the reader's world, then immediately
inverts it. The inversion must imply a larger, counterintuitive
truth that the reader can only access by clicking "more."

Structure: "[Common belief]. [Inversion]."

DO: "Most productivity advice assumes you want to be productive.
That's the mistake."
DON'T: "Have you ever thought about productivity differently?"
DON'T: "In today's world, we need to rethink productivity."

The first sentence should feel like a door closing on an old idea.
The second should feel like a window opening on a new one.
```

**Best for:** ☐ Analytical content ☐ Contrarian content ☐ Personal content
**Emotional register:** Confident. Slightly provocative. Not aggressive.

---

#### Pattern 2: The Specificity Bomb

**The mechanism:** Use an oddly precise number, date, or detail that implies a story too specific to be made up. The brain latches onto precision as a signal of truth and narrative.

**Structure:**
`[Oddly specific number/detail] + [implication of a larger story or consequence]`

**Do example:**
"I tracked every decision I made for thirty days. By day seventeen, I was functionally useless."

**Don't example:**
"I've been thinking a lot about decision fatigue lately." (No specificity. No story implied. No tension.)

**Prompt instruction:**

```
HOOK PATTERN — SPECIFICITY BOMB:
Open with an oddly precise number, date, or concrete detail that
implies a story. The specificity signals "this actually happened"
and creates a curiosity gap: the reader needs to know what happened
next.

Structure: "[Specific number/detail]. [Consequence or implication
that raises a question.]"

DO: "I tracked every decision I made for thirty days. By day
seventeen, I was functionally useless."
DO: "The email arrived at 4:47 p.m. on a Thursday. By Friday,
I'd lost a client."
DON'T: "I've been thinking a lot about decisions lately."
DON'T: "Many people struggle with too many choices."

The number should feel almost uncomfortably specific. Not "a few
weeks" but "seventeen days." Not "recently" but "4:47 p.m. on
a Thursday." Precision is the hook.
```

**Best for:** ☐ Analytical content ☐ Personal content ☐ Contrarian content
**Emotional register:** Matter-of-fact. The specificity does the emotional work.

#### Pattern 3: The Contrarian Cliffhanger

**The mechanism:** State a controversial or surprising position, then stop. Don't explain. Don't justify. The reader's need for resolution forces the click.

**Structure:**
`[Controversial position stated as fact]. [Stop. Or a single word/fragment that deepens the mystery.]`

**Do example:**
"Decision fatigue isn't real. At least, not in the way we've been told."

**Don't example:**
"I have a controversial opinion about decision fatigue." (Announcing the controversy kills it. Just state the position.)

**Prompt instruction:**

```
HOOK PATTERN — CONTRARIAN CLIFFHANGER:
Open with a controversial or surprising position stated as a flat
declaration. Do NOT announce it as controversial ("Hot take:" or
"Unpopular opinion:"). Just state it. Then either stop, or add a
single qualifying fragment that deepens the mystery without
resolving it.

Structure: "[Controversial position]. [Optional: one fragment that
complicates it.]"

DO: "Decision fatigue isn't real. At least, not in the way we've
been told."
DO: "Your morning routine is making you less productive. Not more."
DON'T: "Hot take: decision fatigue is overrated."
DON'T: "I think we might be wrong about decision fatigue."

The reader should feel a small jolt of "you can't just SAY that"
followed immediately by "…but what do you mean?" That gap is the click.
```

**Best for:** ☐ Contrarian content ☐ Analytical content
**Emotional register:** Provocative but calm. Not angry. Not performative.

#### Pattern 4: The Mid-Scene Drop

**The mechanism:** Drop the reader into the middle of a scene with no context. They need to keep reading to understand where they are and what's happening.

**Structure:**
`[In medias res moment. Sensory detail. No setup.]`

**Do example:**
"The cursor blinked. Forty-five minutes. The document was still blank. And I had a board meeting in an hour."

**Don't example:**
"Last week I experienced something that taught me about creativity." (Announces the lesson. Kills the scene.)

**Prompt instruction:**

```
HOOK PATTERN — MID-SCENE DROP:
Open in the middle of a specific scene. No preamble. No "let me
tell you about." Drop the reader into a moment with sensory detail
and implied stakes. They must keep reading to understand context.

Structure: "[Present-tense or immediate-past sensory detail].
[Implied stakes or time pressure.]"

DO: "The cursor blinked. Forty-five minutes. The document was
still blank. And I had a board meeting in an hour."
DON'T: "Last week I learned something important about creative blocks."
DON'T: "We've all stared at a blank page before."

Use fragments. Present tense or immediate past. Physical details.
The reader should feel like they walked into a room mid-conversation.
```

**Best for:** ☐ Personal content ☐ Narrative-driven analytical content
**Emotional register:** Immediate. Visceral. Slightly breathless.

### LINKEDIN HOOK ARCHITECTURES

---

#### Pattern 1: The Insight-First Headline

**The mechanism:** Name a specific professional struggle and imply a framework solution. The reader thinks "that's me" and "there's a fix?" simultaneously.

**Structure:**
`[Specific professional pain] + [implied framework/solution] + [optional: surprising qualifier]`

**Do example:**
"The Three-Step Framework I Use to Cut Daily Decisions by 60% (Without Losing Creative Spontaneity)"

**Don't example:**
"Some thoughts on decision fatigue." (No specificity. No framework implied. No reason to click.)

**Prompt instruction:**

```
HOOK PATTERN — INSIGHT-FIRST HEADLINE (LinkedIn Slide 1):
Write a single headline (6–12 words) that names a specific
professional struggle and implies a framework or solution. The
reader should self-identify ("that's me") AND feel curiosity
("there's a fix?").

Structure: "[Specific pain or outcome] + [implied method] +
[optional surprising qualifier in parentheses]"

DO: "The Three-Step Framework I Use to Cut Daily Decisions by 60%
(Without Losing Creative Spontaneity)"
DO: "Why Your Best Decisions Happen Before 9 a.m. (And How to
Protect That Window)"
DON'T: "Some thoughts on decision fatigue."
DON'T: "5 Tips for Better Decision-Making"

The headline should feel like a colleague leaning over at a
conference and saying, "Hey, I figured something out. Want to see?"
Not a guru on a stage.
```

**Best for:** ☐ Analytical content ☐ Framework-heavy content
**Emotional register:** Professional-peer. Warm. Slightly proud (you solved something).

#### Pattern 2: The Vulnerability Ledger

**The mechanism:** Open with a specific, costly mistake or failure. The specificity makes it real. The cost makes it stakes. The reader thinks "I've done something like that" and needs to know how you recovered.

**Structure:**
`[Specific mistake with a concrete cost] + [time marker] + [implied lesson/system]`

**Do example:**
"Last year, I made a $15,000 decision while mentally depleted. Here's the system that ensured I'd never do it again."

**Don't example:**
"I've made some bad decisions in my career." (Vague. No cost. No specificity. No implied system.)

**Prompt instruction:**

```
HOOK PATTERN — VULNERABILITY LEDGER (LinkedIn Slide 1 / Line 1):
Open with a specific mistake or failure that had a concrete,
quantifiable cost. The specificity makes it real. The cost creates
stakes. Then imply (don't fully reveal) the system or lesson that
emerged.

Structure: "[Time marker], I [specific mistake] that cost [concrete
number/consequence]. Here's [implied system/lesson]."

DO: "Last year, I made a $15,000 decision while mentally depleted.
Here's the system that ensured I'd never do it again."
DO: "I lost my best client in 2023 because I said yes to a meeting
at 4:30 p.m. on a Friday. I never schedule after 3 anymore."
DON'T: "I've made some bad decisions in my career."
DON'T: "Failure is the best teacher. Here's what I learned."

The vulnerability must be SPECIFIC and COSTLY. Not "I struggled."
Not "I learned a lesson." A number. A name. A date. A consequence.
```

**Best for:** ☐ Personal content ☐ Transformation narratives
**Emotional register:** Candid. Slightly wry. "I can laugh about it now" energy.

#### Pattern 3: The Diagnostic Question

**The mechanism:** Ask a question that makes the reader self-diagnose. They answer it internally, realize the answer is uncomfortable, and need to read on for the reframe.

**Structure:**
`"When's the last time you [specific professional action] without [implied problem]?"`

**Do example:**
"When's the last time you made a major decision without checking your phone first? Think about that."

**Don't example:**
"Do you struggle with decision fatigue?" (Too generic. Yes/no answer. No self-diagnosis.)

**Prompt instruction:**

```
HOOK PATTERN — DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION (LinkedIn Slide 1 / Line 1):
Ask a question that forces the reader to self-diagnose a specific
behavior. The question should be narrow enough that the answer
reveals something uncomfortable. Follow with a brief "Think about
that" or "Be honest" beat.

Structure: "When's the last time you [specific action] without
[implied problem]? [Beat: 'Think about that.' / 'Be honest.']"

DO: "When's the last time you made a major decision without
checking your phone first? Think about that."
DON'T: "Do you struggle with decision fatigue?"
DON'T: "Have you ever felt overwhelmed by choices?"

The question must be SPECIFIC (not "do you struggle with X") and
must imply a behavior the reader hasn't examined. The discomfort
of the honest answer is the hook.
```

**Best for:** ☐ Analytical content with a self-awareness angle ☐ Contrarian content
**Emotional register:** Gentle challenge. Not accusatory. "I'm asking myself this too."

### TIKTOK / REELS HOOK ARCHITECTURES

---

#### Pattern 1: The Visual-Verbal Contradiction

**The mechanism:** The spoken words say one thing. The text overlay or visual says the opposite (or complicates it). The brain can't reconcile the two and stops scrolling to resolve the conflict.

**Structure:**
`[Spoken: direct command or statement] + [Visual/text: contradiction or complication]`

**Do example:**
Spoken: "Stop trying to make better decisions."
Text overlay: "Seriously. Stop."
Visual: You physically pushing a decision-making app off your desk.

**Don't example:**
Spoken: "Hey guys, today I want to talk about decision fatigue."
Text overlay: "Decision Fatigue Tips"
(No contradiction. No pattern interrupt. Generic.)

**Prompt instruction:**

```
HOOK PATTERN — VISUAL-VERBAL CONTRADICTION (TikTok 0–1.5 sec):
The spoken hook and the visual/text overlay must create a small
contradiction or tension. The brain stops to resolve the conflict.

Structure:
SPOKEN: [Direct command or surprising statement]
TEXT OVERLAY: [Contradiction, complication, or amplification]
VISUAL: [Physical action that reinforces the tension]

DO:
SPOKEN: "Stop trying to make better decisions."
TEXT: "Seriously. Stop."
VISUAL: Pushing a phone/app off the desk.

DON'T:
SPOKEN: "Hey guys, today I want to talk about decision fatigue."
TEXT: "Decision Fatigue Tips"
VISUAL: Static talking head.

The contradiction should be small but noticeable. Not confusing—
intriguing. The viewer should think "wait, what?" not "I don't
understand."
```

**Best for:** ☐ Contrarian content ☐ Analytical content with a reframe
**Emotional register:** Direct. Slightly confrontational. Playful.

---

#### Pattern 2: The Mid-Action Interrupt

**The mechanism:** The video starts mid-physical-action. No greeting. No setup. The viewer walks into a scene already in progress and needs to stay to understand what's happening.

**Structure:**
`[Video opens mid-physical-action] + [First spoken words are a continuation, not a beginning]`

**Do example:**
Video opens with you mid-sentence, pointing at a whiteboard covered in crossed-out options.
Spoken: "—and THAT is why I stopped making decisions after 2 p.m."

**Don't example:**
Video opens with you sitting, smiling at camera.
Spoken: "Hey! So today I want to share something really important about decision fatigue…"

**Prompt instruction:**

```
HOOK PATTERN — MID-ACTION INTERRUPT (TikTok 0–1.5 sec):
The video opens mid-physical-action. No greeting. No "hey guys."
The first spoken words are a continuation of a thought already in
progress. The viewer walks into a scene and must stay to catch up.

Structure:
VISUAL: [Mid-action: pointing, writing, walking, holding a prop]
SPOKEN: "[Continuation of a thought. Starts mid-sentence or with
'—and THAT is why…']"
TEXT OVERLAY: [Optional: a key phrase from the spoken line]

DO:
VISUAL: Mid-sentence, pointing at a whiteboard.
SPOKEN: "—and THAT is why I stopped making decisions after 2 p.m."

DON'T:
VISUAL: Sitting, smiling at camera.
SPOKEN: "Hey! So today I want to share something about decisions…"

The energy should feel like the viewer interrupted you mid-rant.
Not a performance. A moment they caught.
```

**Best for:** ☐ Personal content ☐ High-energy analytical content
**Emotional register:** Urgent. Mid-thought. Slightly breathless.

### NEWSLETTER SUBJECT LINE ARCHITECTURES

---

#### Pattern 1: The Curiosity Gap

**The mechanism:** Imply a revelation without revealing it. The reader needs to open the email to close the gap.

**Structure:**
`[Specific scene or moment] + [implied revelation withheld]`

**Do example:**
"The Tuesday I couldn't write a single word"

**Don't example:**
"My thoughts on creativity and decision fatigue" (No scene. No gap. No reason to open.)

**Prompt instruction:**

```
SUBJECT LINE — CURIOSITY GAP:
Write a subject line (under 8 words) that implies a specific scene
or moment with an unrevealed outcome. The reader needs to open the
email to find out what happened.

Structure: "[Specific time/scene] + [implied but withheld revelation]"

DO: "The Tuesday I couldn't write a single word"
DO: "What the 4:47 email taught me"
DON'T: "My thoughts on creativity"
DON'T: "This week's video + some thoughts on decisions"

The subject line should feel like the first line of a story someone
started telling you at a dinner party. You NEED to hear the rest.
```

---

#### Pattern 2: The Quiet Confession

**The mechanism:** A small, honest admission that implies vulnerability. The reader opens because it feels like they're being let in on something.

**Structure:**
`"I [small, honest admission that implies a larger story]"`

**Do example:**
"I almost didn't send this one"

**Don't example:**
"An important update about my content" (Corporate. No vulnerability. No story.)

**Prompt instruction:**

```
SUBJECT LINE — QUIET CONFESSION:
Write a subject line (under 7 words) that is a small, honest
admission implying vulnerability or hesitation. The reader opens
because it feels like they're being let in on something private.

Structure: "I [small admission]. / [Implied hesitation.]"

DO: "I almost didn't send this one"
DO: "I was wrong about this"
DO: "The thing I keep avoiding"
DON'T: "An important update"
DON'T: "Some honest thoughts"

The confession should be SMALL. Not dramatic. Not performative.
Just a quiet "hey, this one's a little different." The understatement
is the hook.
```
